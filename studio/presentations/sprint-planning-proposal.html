SafeTapp Mobile Trio · Proposal

Rethinking how we plan sprints.

Our planning meetings run 1–2 hours and most of that time isn't actually planning. This is a proposal to split the work into the right shapes, with clear role expectations, while honouring the reality that BIS is harder to design for than a textbook SaaS.

Authored by Mika For Anita, Aymeric, Udayan Discuss · don't decide

01 · Premise

BIS isn't a textbook SaaS — and our process should reflect that.

Most "how to run a sprint" advice assumes a documented system, a self-service product, and a PM who can fully specify before handoff. None of those are fully true at BIS, and pretending they are is part of why our meetings feel heavy.

The honest premise: the BIS codebase is large, lightly documented, and the behaviour of existing features lives mostly in the heads of the people who built or tested them. Every non-trivial ticket starts with system archaeology before any product work can begin.

That archaeology is real work — but the team doesn't currently name it as work. It happens invisibly inside discovery, inside design, inside dev. When estimates feel wrong and sprints feel heavy, this is usually why.

Every role at BIS runs in two modes

ModeWhat it isWhen it happens
Standard product work Investigate users, design solutions, build, test, ship. Visible. Expected.
System archaeology Figuring out how the existing BIS system already works because there's no spec to read. Invisible. Unaccounted for.

Naming the second mode is the first step. Once we can call it "archaeology," we can estimate it, schedule it, and capture its output as durable documentation instead of throwaway meeting talk.

Why it feels heavy — even though everyone is working hard

None of the four jobs is wrong. Each is real work. The problem is that they belong in different containers:

  • Discovery needs prep, focus, and the right people — not an ambush mid-meeting.
  • Status is async work — a Slack thread does it in 2 minutes instead of 25.
  • Design walkthroughs deserve their own slot so they're not rushed.
  • Sprint planning only works when tickets are already ready. Otherwise it becomes scoping.

And because everything happens at once, sprint planning itself ends up squeezed into the last 5 minutes — which is exactly when we lock in commitments we can't actually deliver.

03 · Proposal

Three smaller meetings, each doing one job well.

Same total time — possibly less. But the work happens in the right container, so each one is faster and clearer.

Meeting 01
Weekly
30–45 min
PM + Trio

Backlog Grooming

Get tickets ready for a future sprint. No commitments made here.

Walk the not-yet-ready list
Identify what each ticket is missing (design, AC, estimate, backend input)
Assign the missing piece with a deadline
No solutioning
No discovery brainstorms
No status updates on in-flight work
Meeting 02
Every 2 weeks
30–45 min · hard cap
Whole trio + QA

Sprint Planning

Pick what fits. Commit. Stop.

Confirm capacity (PTO, partial allocation, holidays)
Walk pre-prioritised list of ready tickets only
Pull from the top until capacity is full
Flag dependencies and risks
No ticket creation
No design walkthroughs
No brainstorming
Meeting 03
As needed
45–90 min
Trio (small group)

Discovery / Trio Brainstorm

Think together in the problem space. Deliberate, with prep.

PM brings a hypothesis (not just a hunch)
Designer brings current-state map of the system area
Dev brings feasibility & system constraints
Output is a decision and a written summary
No status, no admin, no scoping unrelated tickets

Before / After

What we do today

  • One ~2 hour weekly meeting
  • Everything happens at once
  • Brainstorms ambush the agenda
  • Status walk-through ticket-by-ticket
  • Tickets created live, in-meeting
  • Sprint commits made by feel, no capacity check
  • Output: "let's schedule another meeting"

What we'd try

  • Three smaller meetings, one job each
  • Grooming & planning separated
  • Discovery scheduled deliberately, with prep
  • Status moves async (Slack thread)
  • Ticket admin happens outside meetings
  • Sprint commits sized against real capacity
  • Output: decisions, with written artifacts

Note on QA. QA isn't in our weekly today, but sprint planning is where their capacity matters most. The version of "too heavy for QA" we hit recently is exactly what gets solved when QA is in the room to size the test surface — not after the fact.

05 · The archaeology problem

"We need a dev or QA to explain features" — that's a real constraint, not a flaw.

This deserves its own section because it's the thing that makes BIS feel different. In a textbook SaaS, the PM and designer can self-serve: read the spec, look at the data, understand the system. At BIS, the spec doesn't exist for most older features. The only people who can explain how mileage scheduling, attendance widgets, or asset on-demand forms actually behave are the ones who built or tested them.

That means:

  • Asking the dev "how does this work?" is part of the job. It's not a sign of underpreparation.
  • Investigation time has to be factored into estimates — a ticket touching four existing features genuinely takes longer than one touching one.
  • Discovery sessions have to start with "what does it currently do?" before "what should we build?"

What's not okay: running the same investigation twice. If we figure out how mileage scheduling works on Monday and have to figure it out again on Friday, that's preventable waste. The fix is documentation as a byproduct of discovery.

What we systematise over time

HabitWhat it produces
Feature anatomy docs One page per major feature — current behaviour, edge cases, related features. Living docs, contributed to by whoever last touched the area.
Discovery → written summary Every brainstorm ends with a paragraph: problem statement, current state, proposed direction, open questions. Not a recording — a doc.
"How does X work?" gaps tracked If the question gets asked and there's no doc, that's a gap worth a 30-min capture session before the area is touched again.
Estimation includes investigation A ticket's size accounts for "how much archaeology does this need?" — that's how heavy sprints stop being a surprise.

This isn't fast or glamorous — it's compound interest. Six months from now, the trio that does this has a knowledge base. The one that doesn't is still asking Aymeric the same questions every sprint.

06 · Trial run

What we could try first — without a full overhaul.

Three concrete moves we can pilot for one month and reassess. None of them require permission, tools, or political capital.

Split the Monday meeting into two slots

30 min grooming on one day, 30 min planning on another. Use the existing time slot — just two halves with different jobs and a hard agenda for each.

Define what "ready" means

Agree on a short checklist: problem statement, acceptance criteria, design final (if needed), estimate. Tickets that don't pass don't enter sprint planning — they go back to grooming.

Schedule discovery, don't ambush it

When a brainstorm impulse hits mid-meeting, say "let's park it and schedule a focused session." The PM brings a hypothesis, the designer brings current-state context, the dev brings feasibility. 60 min focused beats 90 min unfocused, every time.

What I'm offering on my side: I'll write a short design-handoff summary at the end of each design — "what's the same as the original scope, what changed during design, what was open that I closed." That makes my work visible to dev and QA before estimation, instead of surfacing mid-sprint. That's the trade I'd put on the table.

"Trio before scope. The ticket was a multiple sign-off field. The output was a new tracking module."

— from the brainstorm that worked. The structural goal of this proposal is to make that the rule, not the exception.
