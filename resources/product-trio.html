Field Notes / Vol. 01 — The Product Trio
Internal · Team Learning
A learning nugget

Don't design against the ticket. Design against the problem.

What changed when a designer, a PM, and an engineer started thinking together — before anyone committed to scope.

§ 01 The Shift

Two ways to handle a feature request. Only one of them finds the right thing to build.

The Old Way

Linear handoff. Each role works in its own lane.

PM scopes → Design mocks → Dev builds

Ticket = spec. Solution is already baked in.

The New Way

Trio overlap. Three roles, one shared problem space.

Ticket = hypothesis. The real solution can still surface.

§ 02 What Each Role Brings

The trio works because each role pulls on a different thread of the same problem.

Product Manager
The customer thread
  • Brings the why — who's asking, what they're trying to do, what's at stake
  • Anchors the group when it drifts: "this is what they're using it for"
  • Holds the business radar — feature vs. module, what leadership reacts to
Engineer
The feasibility thread
  • Stress-tests every idea in real time — what breaks, what scales, what's buildable
  • Draws the technical edges so design isn't sketching against thin air
  • Often spots the consolidation move first: "we already have this"
Designer
The pattern thread
  • Holds the canvas — translates the conversation into something the group can react to
  • Zooms out when the room goes deep into edge cases
  • Connects scattered pieces into a single anatomy the trio can see at once
§ 03 Where AI Fits — The Sequencing Rule

AI is an amplifier, not a shortcut. Synthesize first. Generate second.

01 · LISTEN
Hear the trio out
Flows, pain points, scenarios on the board. No visuals yet.
→
02 · SYNTHESIZE
Name 2–3 explicit principles
"Works for scheduled + ad hoc. Accommodates outside visitors. Compliance is non-negotiable. Does that match?"
→
03 · GENERATE
Then bring in Claude
Now the wireframe tests a shared hypothesis instead of creating one.
What we got wrong

We jumped from listen straight to generate. The wireframe was impressive and the room reacted well — but that reaction substituted for alignment that hadn't actually happened yet. A compelling visual can close down exploration before the trio has agreed on what they're solving.

The rule

Generate visuals to test a shared hypothesis — never to create one.
